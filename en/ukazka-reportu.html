This is the plan you receive.

This is a shortened report from a real project. The company's name and its systems are replaced with generic descriptions; the numbers and conclusions are original.

01 - Starting point

What the company was dealing with

A B2B distribution company, around 30 people, over 25 years on the market. Quotes keep the company alive - and every quote starts as an inquiry that passes through four pairs of hands and four programs:

01

An inquiry arrives

The customer sends an email, often with an attachment. The inquiry is registered in an internal quoting application: deadline, quantities, contacts.

02

Suppliers are contacted

The inquiry reaches the person who calculates the price. They email the suppliers.

03

Prices are retyped by hand

Each supplier replies differently: email, PDF, sometimes even SMS. Someone retypes it all into a costing spreadsheet in Excel, where purchase prices become the selling price.

04

A quote is created

The price is transferred from Excel back into the internal application and the quote goes out to the customer.

The owners suspected it was slow, error-prone and eating people's time. They wanted to know where to start.

02 - Findings

Where time gets lost

The workshop revealed three places where the process hurts most:

01

Retyping prices from emails by hand

Supplier replies arrive as emails, PDFs and SMS, and someone retypes them into the costing spreadsheet by hand. The retyping and filling-in takes more time than the price calculation itself. People jump between four programs: email, Excel, the internal quoting application and an older company system.

02

Over five hundred costing spreadsheets, each one different

No single template; the structure differs file by file. Nothing can be automated on top of them in their current form; the spreadsheets would have to be unified first. The count is a workshop estimate; I did not count the files myself.

03

Supplier knowledge only in people's heads

Nowhere is it written down who can manufacture what or how fast each supplier responds. A few people carry it all in their heads - and when one of them leaves, the company loses it.

05 - Where AI does not

Where AI will not help

I said no in two places. Precisely these two "no"s protected the client from the most expensive mistakes:

The price calculation itself

The price here is not a mechanical calculation. Every inquiry is different, and during costing a business decision - and often a new business opportunity - arises. The people who calculate prices protected that space themselves: if a machine built the price, it would disappear. AI may at most suggest from history ("you made a similar thing last year for this much").

Deciding what the quote looks like

Every customer inquires differently: free text, their own spreadsheet, a PDF with pictures. And an email does not reliably reveal what form of quote the customer expects: one large customer wants prices filled into their own spreadsheet, and another with a different one can appear at any time. A mistake here means a lost order. I therefore ruled out two automation ideas entirely: having AI build the quote straight from the inquiry, and having it fill in customer spreadsheets on its own. AI may read and pre-fill - but it must not decide for a person what the quote should look like, and it must not send anything without review.

06 - Recommendation

What to start with

I would start by extracting data from emails straight into the costing - manual retyping currently runs at both ends of the process.

01

The assistant reads the email

An incoming customer inquiry as well as a supplier reply.

02

It extracts the data

Who is inquiring, which items, which prices.

03

It prepares the costing input

A person reviews and approves. Nothing goes out on its own.

+

Why this first

You can start right away and it does not touch the existing systems. If it does not prove itself, you switch it off and the process runs as before.

07 - Outlook

What comes next

The first step saves time immediately, but the data still lives in four places: email, Excel, the quoting application and the older company system. The long-term direction is one place - a single database of inquiries, quotes and price history.

There is more than one way to get there, and money and pace decide. The first step makes sense precisely because it works even if the big rebuild never happens.

08 - Today

How it turned out

The client chose that first step and left the rollout to me. The assistant is now being built under my direction - it reads incoming inquiries and prepares an input that a person only reviews and approves. The report did not end up in a drawer.

The pilot is already running on real data: from an actual inquiry the assistant extracted all 47 items including specifications and prepared the costing spreadsheet. One pilot case proves nothing yet, but this is exactly what the first step is supposed to do.
